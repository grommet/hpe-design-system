Surfaces are the layers of a page, from the page itself to containers such as cards and panels, and temporary elements that appear above them. Each surface holds content or sets it apart from its surroundings. By separating sections and grouping related content, surfaces make pages cleaner, easier to read, and easier to navigate.

To apply this in practice, see [Designing with surfaces](/foundation/designing-with-surfaces).

## What surfaces are for

Surfaces make relationships between parts of a page visible, showing what belongs together, what is separate, and what is asking for attention.

A surface does more than color a background. Placing content on its own surface draws a boundary around it. Contrast between a surface and the one behind it signals *this is a distinct thing*. This is why the system uses surfaces first, only reaching for dividers or borders when a surface alone is not enough. The result is a page with fewer lines on it, and less visual noise for the reader to process.

Structure typically starts with spacing, headings, and layout. Surfaces are added when those are insufficient, and dividers are a last resort when a boundary needs further emphasis.

A floating layer sits over the page rather than in it, so that a menu, a dialog, or a tooltip can appear without disturbing the layout beneath. See [what counts as floating](#what-counts-as-floating).

## Root surfaces and tones

**Root surfaces** are the starting surfaces upon which content and other surfaces sit. `background.surface.base` is the page or canvas. `background.surface.floating` is for modals, drawers, menus, and tooltips.

**Surface tones** are used on containers needing grouping, separation, or emphasis. These are `background.surface.tone.1` and `background.surface.tone.2`.

### Nesting tones

Nested containers alternate between `background.surface.tone.1` and `background.surface.tone.2`. Tone 1, then tone 2, then tone 1 again, as deep as the content goes: the pair works indefinitely, and nothing runs out.

*Tones can be used on `background.surface.base` or `background.surface.floating` and alternate and nest indefinitely. The first container on either takes `background.surface.tone.1`.*

### A note on the numbers

The tones are peers. Neither is deeper than the other, and the numbers are labels rather than levels. `background.surface.tone.2` is not "level two". What decides which tone applies is what the container is sitting on, not how far down it is. The one fixed point is the start: the first container on the page or on a floating layer always takes `background.surface.tone.1`, so `background.surface.tone.2` never sits directly on either.

Surfaces are chosen by asking what the content needs: "Does this need grouping, separation, or emphasis?" not by asking "How far down the tree does it sit?"

### What counts as floating

**In the page, or over it?**

A floating element appears over the page rather than in it. A menu opens on top of whatever is beneath it. A modal covers the page until you deal with it. A tooltip sits over the content it describes.

The clearest test is whether it fits the layout. A container has to fit the space its layout gives it. A floating layer does not. It can be larger than the thing that opened it, and can sit over content it has nothing to do with. If it stays inside the area it belongs to and moves and scrolls with the content around it, it is a container: give it a tone. If it breaks out over the top of everything else, it is floating.

A navigation rail, a contextual side panel, and an inspector all take up space in the layout: content sits alongside them, not underneath. They take a tone, however panel-like they look.

Because a floating layer is a root surface, the tones start again from it. A container inside a modal takes `background.surface.tone.1`, exactly as it would on the page. The same component works in both places without knowing where it is.

## What is not a surface

A surface is a structural fill: the page, a container, or a floating layer. It takes part in the alternation, and it changes when the surface values change. Other background design tokens do neither. They are chosen for what they mean, not for what they sit on.

| Need | Use | Why it is not a surface |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A fill that carries meaning, such as a status banner or alert region | A status background such as `background.info` or `background.critical` | It signals a state, not a boundary. |
| An emphasis or promotional panel | An accent background such as `background.accent.blue.weak`, used sparingly | It draws attention. It does not group or separate. |
| A small fill inside a component, such as a table header band, a card footer, or a chip | `background.contrast` | It is local to the component and takes no part in the page's layering. |
| Chrome that must stand apart from a `background.surface.tone.1` panel | A named background design token outside the surface set. Raise it with the design system team rather than improvising a third tone. | It is a deliberate exception, not a third tone. |

None of these alternate, and none is affected by a change to the surface values.

`background.default` is not a surface either. It is retained for existing work and takes no part in the alternation; for a page, a container, or a floating layer, use the surface design tokens.

### Fills inside a component

A band, footer, or header inside a component is usually a small fill doing local work, so it takes a standard background design token such as `background.contrast`. Only where a region inside a component genuinely reads as a container in its own right does it take a surface tone, whichever tone the component is not. The component resolves this itself: apply a surface tone to the component, never to its parts.
